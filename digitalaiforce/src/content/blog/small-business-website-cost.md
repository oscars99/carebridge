“How much does a website cost?” is the first question almost every small business owner asks — and the honest answer is: **it depends on what you need the website to do.** A simple online brochure and a lead-generating sales machine are very different projects.

This guide breaks down the real options, what drives the price up or down, the ongoing costs people forget about, and how to make sure you get a website that pays for itself.

## The short answer

For most small businesses, a professionally built website lands somewhere between **the low thousands and the low five figures**, depending on size, features and who builds it. DIY website builders cost less in cash but more in time, and they come with real limits.

The better question isn’t “what’s the cheapest website?” but **“what will bring in customers for a fair price?”** A website that generates two extra jobs a month is worth far more than one that simply exists.

## Your three main options

| Option | Typical upfront cost | Best for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **DIY website builder** | Low (monthly subscription) | Very early-stage businesses testing an idea | Your time, generic templates, limited SEO and speed control |
| **Freelancer** | Moderate | Simple sites with a clear scope | Availability, quality varies, gaps in SEO/copy/support |
| **Agency** | Moderate to higher | Businesses that want leads, rankings and ongoing support | Opaque pricing, long contracts, not owning your site |

### DIY website builders

Platforms like Wix or Squarespace let you build a site yourself for a monthly fee. They’re a reasonable starting point if money is very tight — but they’re not free. Expect to spend many hours on design, writing and setup, and you may hit walls with speed, search optimization and custom features. Moving off the platform later can also mean starting over.

### Freelancers

A good freelancer can deliver a solid website at a fair price, especially for smaller projects. The trade-off is that one person rarely covers design, copywriting, SEO, speed optimization and long-term support equally well — and you depend on their availability after launch.

### Agencies

An agency brings a team: strategy, design, development, copywriting and SEO under one roof, plus ongoing support. The best agencies give you a fixed written quote, explain what’s included and make sure you own everything. The ones to avoid lock you into long contracts or keep control of your domain and website.

## What actually drives the price

Every website quote comes down to a handful of factors:

- **Number of pages.** A 5-page site (home, about, services, contact, one more) costs less than 20 pages with individual service and location pages — but those extra pages are often what helps you rank on Google.
- **Custom design vs. template.** A custom design that reflects your brand takes more time than tweaking a theme, but it builds more trust and converts better.
- **Copywriting.** Clear, persuasive words are one of the biggest drivers of results. Writing them yourself saves money; having professionals write them usually pays off.
- **Features and integrations.** Online booking, quote calculators, e-commerce, CRM connections, member areas and AI chat assistants all add development time.
- **SEO setup.** Proper page structure, metadata, schema markup and speed optimization are essential if you want to be found — and they’re often missing from bargain builds.
- **Content and photos.** Professional photography, stock images, videos or migrating content from an old site all affect the scope.
- **Timeline.** Rush projects require more resources at once.

## The ongoing costs people forget

The launch price is only part of the picture. Plan for:

- **Domain name** — renewed every year, usually a modest fee.
- **Hosting** — from free (on some modern platforms) to a monthly fee for managed, high-performance hosting.
- **Maintenance and security** — updates, backups and monitoring, either done by you or covered by a care plan.
- **Content updates** — new photos, services, offers and blog posts keep your site fresh and help SEO.
- **Marketing** — a website doesn’t get traffic on its own. SEO, Google Business Profile, ads and email are what bring visitors in.

## The hidden cost of a “cheap” website

A low price can get expensive if the site:

- loads slowly, so visitors leave before it finishes loading;
- looks broken or cramped on phones, where most local searches happen;
- isn’t structured for search engines, so nobody finds it;
- hides the phone number and has no clear call to action;
- can’t be updated without paying a developer every time; or
- needs to be completely rebuilt in a year or two.

Any one of those can cost you more in lost customers than you saved on the build.

## Think about return, not just cost

Here’s a simple way to sanity-check any quote. Ask yourself: **what is one new customer worth to my business?** Then estimate how many extra customers a better website might bring in each month.

If a new customer is worth $500 and an improved site brings in just two extra customers a month, that’s $1,000 a month — or $12,000 a year — from the same investment. Your numbers will be different, but the exercise shows why a website that converts is worth paying for properly.

## Questions to ask before you hire anyone

1. **Who will own the domain, website and content?** (The answer should be: you.)
2. **What exactly is included** — pages, copywriting, SEO setup, forms, analytics?
3. **Is the quote fixed**, and what would change it?
4. **Will it be fast and mobile-friendly?** Ask to see the speed scores of recent sites.
5. **Can I update it myself**, and will you show me how?
6. **What happens after launch?** Is there support, and what does it cost?
7. **Are there long-term contracts** or cancellation fees?

## The bottom line

A small business website is an investment in your most important sales tool. Choose the option that fits your goals and budget today, insist on owning what you pay for, and make sure the site is built to be found and to convert.
